# The Sable Recollections

Status: canonical supporting lore

*The private memoir of Duke Alvra Sable, dictated in her ninth decade and
recovered from her estate archive after her death. It is one character's
account: it gives the [world bible](galactic-lore.md) a voice and adds no
facts to it. Where the two disagree, the world bible wins.*

---

## I. The Proclamation

I was twenty-six when the Proclamation reached our holding, and I remember
being furious about it. Not afraid. Furious. My father had spent thirty
years building a shipping concern out of nothing, and the Court wanted his
heir to walk away from it and dig in the dirt of some frontier rock nobody
had bothered to name, because it was time for another Contest.

We called it a game, in private, when the stewards weren't listening.
Everyone in the Core did. They followed a Sector Contest the way my
grandmother's generation had followed the war reports: with wagers, and
favourites, and a great many opinions about ground they would never stand
on. It took me longer than I'd like to admit to understand that calling it
a game and understanding what it actually was were two different things,
and that I could do both at once and still be right to go.

The Writ of Sectors is short enough to embroider on a cushion, and my aunt
had. *To hold nothing is to owe everything.* I grew up thinking that was a
line about duty. It is a line about debt, and it means exactly what it
says.

The survey packet called our Sector nothing at all: coordinates, a soil
report, a list of harbours. I named it Halveth after my mother, once I'd
won it, which tells you how this story ends. It does not tell you what it
cost, so I will.

## II. What the Towns Knew

The House sent me down with one Fabrication Complex, the standard
equipment and a few sealed charges from the family. Everything else I
was expected to take from the world itself. We did not land alone. The
Court puts its Houses down together, on the same ground, and lets the
ground decide.

The first town we found ran on burning fuel. Actual combustion: carts and
generators coughing black smoke into the square. A morning's walk outside
it stood a Concordance ziggurat, sealed and dark, with goats grazing up to
the edge of its paving. I asked the town's elder why, with a machine like
that on her doorstep, her people had never lit so much as a lamp from it.

She looked at me the way you look at someone who has clearly never gone
hungry, and said: "Because whatever's still hungry out here already ate
it."

I took that for superstition. It was history. Her grandmother's
grandmother had watched the Wardens come for every town that kept its
aether-work running, and the survivors had drawn the sensible conclusion.
For generations these people had kept themselves alive by being too plain
to notice. Then we arrived, with the Court's licence to be noticed on
their behalf.

There was a second thing she did not say, which I had later from a Court
surveyor who had drunk too much. The world had had a government, the year
before we landed. The Court had removed it, so that we would find the
place tidy.

## III. The Holdouts

Not all of it had agreed to be removed.

The Court's name for what was left was Planetary Defense, which is a
generous name for soldiers with no government to answer to, dug into their
old barracks and depots, declining to notice that the war had been settled
over their heads. They could not recruit. They could not be resupplied.
Every camp we took was a camp that would never exist again, and they knew
that better than we did.

There was a camp in the hills east of our first Crystal seam. For a month
it did nothing at all, and my captain advised me to let it go on doing
nothing. I was twenty-six, so I had my command study it instead: its
pickets, its sight lines, the hours its lamps were lit. On the eleventh
night it came down the hill.

I learned afterwards that this is the one thing they have left: old
sensors that can tell when they are being looked at. A camp that is
watched wakes. Nobody had thought to tell a twenty-six-year-old heir.

We held the seam. We lost the eastern beacon, and with it every field it
had anchored. In the morning I walked the ground. Beam weapons leave
furrows of dark glass, still warm. The dead in the furrows wore the crest
of a government that no longer existed, and several of them were older
than my father.

There is a bounty for a taken camp, if your House has filed the right
commission. Ours had. I signed for it. The Court had cleared the world for
us. Nobody had asked the world.

## IV. What It Announces

We garrisoned the ziggurat in the third month and woke what was under it.
I will not describe the works. It is enough to say that it did in a day
what my fabricators did in a month, and that nothing the Court's own
foundries can build comes close. I remember the sheer relief of it. Real
power, real growth, the thing that would let Halveth become something
instead of a camp.

Nobody warns you what waking one announces. A working relic leaves a wake
in the aether that every House on the world can read: where it is, and
whose codes are driving it. I had lit a lamp in a dark room full of people
holding knives.

Within the week, two Houses that had never troubled us found reasons to.

That is the actual shape of what the Writ asks of you. Not bravery in the
abstract. The specific, repeated willingness to keep lighting lamps.

## V. The Factory

I will tell you about the factory, because the histories of Halveth do
not.

A House fights with ancillaries: bodies, directed from orbit by the
House's own machine mind. They do not tire and they do not desert. They
have to come from somewhere, and on a frontier world there is only one
place they can come from. The towns had people. The factory made them
into something else.

We entered them in the ledgers as levies. The towns had another word, and
stopped using it when I was in the room.

I signed the first order in the fourth month, when the House to our north
was two days from our harbour and I had nothing else to fill the line
with. I have heard Dukes say they had no choice. I had a choice. I could
have lost.

The elder came to the gate that week. She did not ask for anyone back.
She asked me whether they knew. I told her I did not know. That was true,
and I have never tried to find out.

## VI. Winning It

I will not recount the whole campaign; the histories that matter are kept
elsewhere, and I was, by the end, tired in a way I have not fully
recovered from even now. I will say only that we won it by the towns,
because that was the way my mother would have chosen: hold more of them
than anyone else, keep them fed, keep them ours. The day I stood in the
central square of what I would come to call Halveth and heard the herald
read out my new rank, I felt something I have chased, uselessly, in every
year since. Not triumph. Relief. The specific relief of a debt, finally,
called paid.

I did not understand, that day, that a Duke's debts are never called paid.
Only refinanced.

In the Core, I am told, they cheered. They had been watching the whole
time. The factory was not in the reports.

## VII. The Weight of Standing

A Duke does not hold a town. A Duke holds a system: Halveth, its moons,
the belt, the gas giant, and the Fighters that keep them. The Court
offered me its protection for the first years in exchange for a pledge not
to move against it. I gave the pledge without reading it twice. I had no
intention of moving against anyone.

The Wardens found us the year the protection lapsed. I had read about
Wardens the way you read about weather on another world. They are older
than the Court. The Concordance built them to burn the Bleed out of open
space, and they did, and then they could not tell the Bleed from the hands
that had made them. Everything aether-built looks the same to a Warden.
Halveth, by then, was very aether-built.

The elder had been dead ten years. I heard her anyway.

Halveth grew regardless. I will spare you the ledgers. What I will tell
you, because the official record leaves it out, is what it felt like to
watch my holdings become large enough that other Dukes stopped
congratulating me and started asking careful questions about my
intentions.

I did not seek a coalition. My accountant built one for me, quietly, out
of trade agreements that made sense on their own terms long before either
of us called them what they were. I want to be honest about that, in case
some future reader imagines me plotting from the first year. I was not. I
was running a shipping concern that had grown teeth.

The Court noticed before I did. That is, I have come to believe, always
true.

Its first answer came in a plain envelope, by a courier who would not sit.
A Writ of Advancement: a ship, free, or a strike on a rival of my
choosing, flown under my own colours so that nobody need ever know whose
it was. I was asked for nothing in return. A Writ is never forced. That is
the cruelty of it. I accepted one, once, and I will not say which. I will
say that I have sat in the Senate across from every Duke of my generation,
knowing each of them had opened the same envelope, and that none of us
ever said so.

Its second answer was silence. In a good year, the best I ever had, the
Court's patrols stopped coming. Nobody announces a Blind Eye. Your weakest
world begins to slip, grievances against you cost nothing to lodge, and
old friends lodge them. I lent the Court ships for its patrols until it
remembered me fondly again. I understood then that I had been the camp in
the hills all along: left in peace exactly as long as I was not worth
studying.

## VIII. The Audience

I was summoned to the Core in my forty-first year, ostensibly to be
honoured for fifteen years of holding Halveth steady. I went expecting a
ceremony. What I remember instead is standing on a balcony I was told had
stood since before the Writ existed, looking out over a horizon of
governed worlds that did not end where my eye expected it to end, and
understanding, in a way no ledger had ever managed to teach me, exactly
how small ten worlds actually are.

I had spent fifteen years telling myself a story about how far Halveth
had come. I told a different story on the transport home. Whatever I had
built, it was not, and was never going to be, a rival to what I had just
seen. I do not think the Court staged that audience to teach me that. I
think it simply forgot that not every Duke already knows it.

## IX. What I Would Tell Halveth's Heir

If you are reading this because you have inherited what I built, or
because you are about to walk into your own Proclamation with your
father's fury still hot in your chest the way mine was, I will not tell
you not to go. I will tell you only what I wish someone had told me.

The Wardens do not hate you. They cannot tell you from the thing they
were built to burn, and they will come for exactly as much as you have
built, no more and no less, for as long as you keep building. That part is
honest, at least. It is the only honest thing out there.

The Court is a different matter. It let me believe, for fifteen years,
that Halveth was something to be reckoned with, and then let one afternoon
on a balcony correct me, without warning and without unkindness. It will
never tell you in advance how far you are from mattering to it. I do not
know if that is cruelty or simple indifference. I have turned the question
over for sixty years and I no longer expect to land on an answer I trust.

Go and stand in the factory once, before you sign anything. I never did.

What I do know is this. Every Duke who has ever quietly wondered whether
the whole arrangement — the Writ, the Contests, a crown that has never
once changed hands by any means a Duke controls — was built to serve them
or to serve something above them, was asking the only question in this
galaxy that has ever mattered. They say that if the Court ever breaks, the
throne will go not to whoever broke it but to whoever holds the most when
it falls. I believe it. Even its own ending has been let out on contract.

I did not have the courage, in my time, to do more than wonder, and I
never forgot, after that balcony, how far even my wondering would have had
to travel before it meant anything to the Court at all. It would take more
than one Duke's wondering to close that distance. I understood that much,
at least, before the end.

I hope, whoever you are, that you do.

*— A. Sable*
