# The Sable Recollections

*Being the private memoir of Duke Alvra Sable, dictated in her ninth decade,
recovered from her estate archive after the events of the Culling of the
Nine Hundredth Cycle. Presented here as an in-world document — a companion
to `galactic-lore.md`, not a replacement for it. Nothing in this text should
be read as settled canon beyond what that document already establishes;
where the two disagree, the worldbible wins.*

---

## I. The Landing

I was twenty-six when the Proclamation reached our holding, and I remember
being furious about it. Not afraid — furious. My father had spent thirty
years building a shipping concern out of nothing, and the Writ wanted me to
walk away from it to go dig in the dirt of some frontier rock nobody had
bothered to name yet, because the Court had decided it was time for another
game.

That's what we called it, in private, when the stewards weren't listening.
A game. It took me longer than I'd like to admit to understand that calling
it a game and understanding what it actually was were two different things,
and that I could do both at once and still be right to go.

The Sector they opened for us was called, in the survey packet, nothing at
all — a string of coordinates and a soil report. I named it Halveth after
my mother, once I'd won it, which tells you how that story ends, but I'm
getting ahead of myself.

We landed in the calm season, which I'm told is not a coincidence — the
Court doesn't open a Sector until its own people have judged it survivable
enough for a first camp. I did not appreciate at the time how much
generosity that represented, nor how little of it would be extended to me
again.

## II. What the Towns Knew

The first town we found ran on burning fuel. Actual combustion — carts and
generators coughing black smoke into air that, three days' walk further
out, would have killed us both in an hour without a beacon between us and
it. I asked the town's elder why, when the Court's own archives are so full
of clean aether-work, her people hadn't so much as a single working relic
of it.

She looked at me the way you look at someone who has clearly never gone
hungry, and said: "Because whatever's still hungry out here already ate
it."

I did not understand her then. I understand her now better than I would
like to.

## III. The First Site

We found our first Crystal seam in the second month, and I remember the
sheer relief of it — real fuel, real growth, the thing that would let
Halveth become something instead of a camp. I did not think, mining it,
about what mining it announced. Nobody warns you about that. The Writ tells
you to hold nothing is to owe everything; it does not mention that
everything you hold starts, from the moment you hold it, to attract
attention you did not ask for.

The first attack came eleven nights after we lit the Synthesizer. It did
not come as an army. It came as a wrongness in the ground near the eastern
seam — soil that moved when nothing was moving it, that built itself, over
the course of an hour, into something with a shape and a hunger. My
garrison captain called it an infestation, used the word like she'd said it
before, and I understood then that this had happened to other Dukes, in
other Sectors, and that nobody had thought to tell a twenty-six-year-old
heir it was coming.

We held the seam. We lost the eastern watchtower. I learned, that night,
the actual shape of the thing the Writ asks of you — not bravery in the
abstract, but the specific, repeated willingness to keep feeding a fire
that keeps drawing wolves.

## IV. Winning It

I will not recount the whole campaign; the histories that matter are kept
elsewhere, and I was, by the end, tired in a way I have not fully recovered
from even now. I will say only that we took the Town Control path, because
it was the one my mother would have chosen, and that the day I stood in the
central square of what I would come to call Halveth and heard the herald
read out my new rank, I felt something I have chased, uselessly, in every
year since. Not triumph. Relief. The specific relief of a debt, finally,
called paid.

I did not understand, that day, that a Duke's debts are never called paid.
Only refinanced.

## V. The Weight of Standing

Halveth grew. I will spare you the ledgers — they are recorded properly
elsewhere, and I was never as proud of the arithmetic as I was of the
people who made it work. What I will tell you, because I think it is the
part the official record leaves out, is what it felt like to watch my own
holdings become large enough that other Dukes stopped congratulating me and
started asking careful questions about my intentions.

I did not seek a coalition. My accountant built one for me, quietly, out of
trade agreements that made sense on their own terms long before either of
us called them what they were. I want to be honest about that, in case some
future reader imagines me plotting from the first Cycle. I was not. I was
running a shipping concern that had grown teeth.

The Court noticed before I did. That is, I have come to believe, always
true.

## VI. The Warning

They took Merit's Reach from me in my forty-first year — not a war, not a
vote, simply a notice, and then it was no longer mine. Ten Planets, the
notice said, as if reciting a fact everyone already knew, is nothing on a
galactic scale. I believed that line for exactly as long as it took me to
understand it wasn't about the ten Planets at all. It was about the fact
that I had stopped looking, to them, like one more Duke among many, and
started looking like a shape they recognized from older records — the
shape of someone who might, eventually, ask them a question they did not
want asked.

I did not resist the taking. I had seen, by then, what happens to the
Dukes who do.

## VII. What I Would Tell Halveth's Heir

If you are reading this because you have inherited what I built, or
because you are about to walk into your own Proclamation with your
father's fury still hot in your chest the way mine was, I will not tell you
not to go. I will tell you only what I wish someone had told me:

The Bleed does not hate you. It does not know you exist until you make it
know, and then it will come for exactly as much as you have made yourself
worth coming for, no more and no less, forever, for as long as you keep
building. That part is honest, at least. It is the only honest thing out
there.

The Court is a different matter. It will smile at your first Planet and
narrow its eyes at your fifth, and it will never, not once, tell you in
advance which Cycle is the one where the arithmetic tips. I do not know if
that is cruelty or simple necessity — I have turned the question over for
sixty years and I no longer expect to land on an answer I trust.

What I do know is this: every Duke who has ever quietly wondered whether
the whole arrangement — the Writ, the culling, the crown nobody is allowed
to actually keep — was built to serve them or to serve something above
them, was asking the only question in this galaxy that has ever actually
mattered. I did not have the courage, in my time, to do more than wonder
it.

I hope, whoever you are, that you do.

*— A. Sable*
